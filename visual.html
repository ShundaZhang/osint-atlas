02 / FIELD NOTES

Read the scene as a graph of relationships

“Across from” is often more useful than “looks similar”.

Describe relations that survive viewpoint changes: a road crosses a river before reaching a T-junction; a long building lies between rail and water; a tower is behind the bridge from the camera. Turn these into a sketch before searching a map.

Distances in a photograph are perspective-dependent. A near object may dominate the image while a distant skyline is compressed. Use relative order and occlusion first; estimate distances only when the scene has a usable scale or a calibrated viewpoint.

03 / FIELD NOTES

Transcribe uncertainty instead of inventing letters

A partial sign can be searched without pretending it is complete.

Write clear characters literally and mark unreadable segments, such as “BRI[?]GE”. Compare alternate alphabets and local transliterations. Use OCR as a suggestion and retain the original crop. A chain logo may narrow a country but seldom uniquely identifies a branch.

Clothing, vegetation and architecture are probabilistic clues, not national identifiers. Ask how widely a feature occurs and whether another independent feature supports the same region. Do not let a confident language guess override a contradictory coastline.
