01 / FIELD NOTES

A skyline is a sequence of relative shapes

Match peak order, saddles and apparent height ratios.

Trace the horizon separately from foreground buildings and clouds. Compare a sequence of peaks and valleys, not one triangular hill. Apparent heights depend on distance and camera elevation; a closer low ridge may hide a taller distant peak.

PeakVisor and terrain views can suggest candidates. In QGIS, combine elevation data, bearing and a plausible viewpoint. Retain several candidate ridges until the relative profile and surrounding roads agree. A named peak label is a lead, not proof.

02 / FIELD NOTES

Visibility is a constraint with assumptions

A DEM, observer height and target height define a simplified model.

A terrain line-of-sight test asks whether modeled ground blocks a target. Add observer height relative to the data surface and account for the target’s height. Buildings and vegetation may be missing or represented differently. A visible target in a model may still be obscured in the photograph.

For distant horizons, curvature, refraction and vertical reference become relevant. Start with a rough compatibility test and state its assumptions; do not describe a low-resolution model as centimetre-accurate surveying.

03 / FIELD NOTES

Check why a terrain match might be misleading

The candidate should explain foreground and background together.

Telephoto compression, a tilted camera and a mirrored image can distort a casual comparison. Lens distortion may change edges; a cropped frame can remove the most distinctive saddle. Inspect several frames or uncropped versions when available.

A good terrain candidate also fits the road direction, water position and camera-side occlusion. If the skyline agrees but the only plausible access road is on the wrong side, keep the contradiction rather than dismissing it as a small detail.
